Steve,

* What I set out to do

Back in Letter 6 I built a second carrier, the accumulating one, because
the ordinary =expected= can hold exactly one error and a validation form
with three bad fields wants to tell you about all three. Then I wrote
nineteen more letters and never once traversed it.

That's an embarrassing gap, and it's the specific kind worth noticing: I
built the thing, proved its applicative laws, proved it isn't a monad,
proved the projection back to the single-error carrier is a morphism, and
never applied it to a list. Collecting independent failures over a
sequence of independent checks is the entire reason the carrier exists.
Every traversal in this model so far has been the short-circuiting one.

So: traverse the accumulating carrier, and prove three things about what
comes out. Which errors, in which order, and what the first one is.

* What the checker refused

The first refusal was structural and I should have seen it coming. I put
the definitions in the accumulating carrier's own file, where they
obviously belong, and Lean rejected the import. The morphism module
imports the accumulating carrier, and the sufficient-grade module imports
the morphism module, so the accumulating carrier cannot see
=traverseK=. And it needs to, because the theorem I actually care about
compares the two traversals. A new module was the honest fix, not the
convenient one.

The second was the law inventory, and it turned into the finding. The
script reads every theorem and records which grade property the proof
consumes, and it flagged fourteen of mine as consuming nothing at all. I
went through them expecting sloppy statements and found instead that the
flag was correct, and interesting. Here are the two reduction
lemmas for the both-failed case, the old one and the new one:

#+begin_src lean
theorem ap_ok_errs  ... = .errs es hne (fun e he => Grade.le_join_right g h (hmem e he))
theorem apK_ok_errs ... = .errs es hne (fun e he => hh (hmem e he))
#+end_src

The first line is the union-graded version: to put the argument's errors
into the result grade it cites =le_join_right=, a named fact about the
grade's order, because it computed the union itself and has to justify
membership in it. The second is the sufficient-grade version, and it uses
=hh=, the inclusion proof the caller handed in. No named fact, because
there's nothing left to justify. The inventory tags the first =order= and
the second nothing, and that difference is the whole sufficient-grade
story showing up in a table instead of in prose.

The accumulation-order theorem is the same shape. It costs no grade
property whatever, because it's a claim about appending lists and the
grade never moves.

* What changed

=apK= for the accumulating carrier is defined by matching on both
arguments and concatenating. It can't be built the way the
short-circuiting one is, out of =bindK=, because there is no monad here
to build it from. That was Letter 6's finding and it's still the reason:
the both-failed case is the one thing sequencing can't produce.

The traversal itself is shaped like its short-circuiting counterpart, and
three theorems say what it does. Every failing position contributes its
errors, in source order, exactly once, which I stated as: the error list
of the traversal is the concatenation of each element's error list, left
to right. An all-succeeding traversal is the mapped list. And the one I
wanted:

Taking the first error of an accumulated traversal is the same as
short-circuiting at the first failure from the start. No side condition,
no hypothesis about how many things failed.

That falls out of a fact I proved in Letter 6 without noticing what it
was for. The accumulating combine puts the function's errors first, and
the short-circuiting one keeps the function's error when the function
fails. Those are the same tie-break, made independently in two places, and
lifting them through a list is an induction with nothing in it.

I wrote the tests with two distinguishable failures instead of one
repeated one, deliberately. With a single error kind the ordering claim
cannot fail. So the guards run =[0, 5, 200]= and get parse
then range, and =[200, 5, 0]= and get range then parse. If I had used the
same kind twice, both would have passed against a broken traversal.

* Back in C++

If you offer both a validating =transpose= and a short-circuiting one,
somebody is going to ask whether they agree. The answer is yes, and the
reason is not that anyone maintained a convention.

The accumulating version appends left to right. The short-circuiting one
stops at the leftmost failure. So the first element of the accumulated
list is the error the short-circuiting version would have returned, by
construction. You get to say: run the validating form when you want the
whole report, run the fast form when you want the first problem, and the
first problem is the same problem either way.

What that costs you is that the order is now a contract. It's observable,
through exactly that projection, so it is not free to change later. If
anyone ever wants an unordered bag of errors, that's a different carrier
with its own projection policy, not this one with a weaker promise.
