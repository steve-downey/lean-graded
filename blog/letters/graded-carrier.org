Steve,

* What I set out to do

Last time I built the grade, the model of =error_set=. This letter builds
the thing the grade decorates: the carrier itself, the model of
=expected<T, error_set<Es...>>=. In Lean it's a two-constructor type: =ok=
holds a value of your result type, =err= holds one error value together
with a proof that its kind is a member of the grade =g=. That "together
with a proof" is the whole model of "one error whose type is in the set":
membership in a =Finset= standing in for what C++ gets from its type
system directly.

The reason this letter exists on its own, rather than being folded into
the grade letter, is a decision your design already made and I wanted to
test honestly rather than assume: at grade ∅, =expected<T, error_set<>>=
is deliberately *not* the type you get; you get bare =T= instead, for API
ergonomics. That's two different C++ types standing in for "no possible
error," related by an implicit conversion you trust without being asked to
prove anything. Lean doesn't let me get away with trusting. =Graded ∅ α=
and =α= are, inescapably, two different inductive types: one of them has
an =err= constructor sitting right there in its definition, unreachable or
not. So the honest question is: what is the relationship? Not "are they
the same" (they aren't) but "how strongly can I say they behave the
same." Lean's answer for that is an =Equiv=, a proven bijection with both
directions checked, and further, a proof that the bijection commutes with
=map= (mapping before or after the ∅-collapse gives the same answer). I
also added =cast= for reinterpreting a carrier across a grade *equality*
(later steps state laws through it), and a =DecidableEq= instance, and I
introduced this step's actual consumer: a two-stage string-parse-then-
range-check validator, composed by hand, so the carrier's shape gets
exercised by real code immediately instead of sitting untested until the
monad laws arrive.

* What the checker refused

The =Equiv= proof itself was exactly as short as the step file predicted:
one line for the forward direction (=absurd= on the impossible membership
proof), one line back, done. That part wasn't a fight; it was the reward
for having phrased the question as "isomorphic," which only asks the
=err= case to be uninhabited, not for the types to unify.

The actual friction was more mundane and, in hindsight, obvious. I wrote
=map=, its two laws, =emptyEquiv=, and =cast= all referring to grade and
type variables (=g=, =α=, =β=, =γ=) that I had never told Lean existed as
free variables of the section. Lean's answer wasn't "undefined variable,"
it was "typeclass instance problem is stuck on a metavariable," because
without a =variable= declaration it tried to silently auto-bind them and
then couldn't figure out what type they were at. The fix was one line: a
=variable {g g' g'' : Grade Err} {α β γ : Type v}=. But the error message
pointed at =DecidableEq= resolution, three definitions downstream of the
real problem, which cost more time than it should have.

The second refusal was in =cast= itself. I first wrote it as =h ▸ ·=
(substitute along the equality, applied to "whatever comes in"), and
composing two casts together produced "application type mismatch," Lean
unable to see through the point-free =·= to know what it was substituting
into. Writing it with an explicit argument, =h ▸ x=, fixed it outright;
the term-mode rewrite notation insists on seeing the actual term it's
rewriting rather than a promise of one arriving later.

The third was self-inflicted redundancy: I gave the =DecidableEq= instance
its own =[DecidableEq Err]= parameter on top of the one already in scope
from the section variables, and got "synthesized instance not
definitionally equal": two different instances of the same fact, neither
one winning at reduction time. Deleting the redundant parameter, so there
was only one =DecidableEq Err= to find, was the whole fix.

* What changed

=Graded/Carrier.lean= adds the =Graded= type, =map= with =map_id= and
=map_comp=, =emptyEquiv= with its naturality lemma =map_emptyEquiv=,
=cast= with =cast_rfl= and =cast_cast=, and the =DecidableEq= instance.
=Examples/Validation.lean= adds the consumer: =parseNat= and =checkRange=,
each producing a single-error grade, and =validate=, which composes them
by hand with a =match= into the union grade =\{parse, range\}=, marked
=REPLACED-BY: bind= at the definition, since that hand-assembly of
membership proofs is exactly the boilerplate a real =bind= exists to
absorb. Three =#eval=s confirm it on a good, a bad, and an out-of-range
string. =Tests/Carrier.lean= instantiates the laws and =#guard=s the
validator's three outcomes.

* Back in C++

The =DecidableEq= instance turned out to carry its own small lesson: the
membership proof inside =err= is a =Prop=, and Lean treats all proofs of
the same proposition as equal to each other automatically ("proof
irrelevance"), so two =err= values compare equal exactly when their error
values do, full stop; the instance doesn't even need to look at *how*
each one proved membership. That is precisely how C++ variant equality
already behaves, just for a reason that looks different from this side:
=error_set= membership is a compile-time fact erased before your program
runs, so there is no "which membership witness" left at runtime to
disagree about. Lean has to say this out loud as a principle about proofs;
C++ gets the same answer by having already thrown the evidence away. And
the bigger point of the letter: being able to make that claim here, and
mean it (mean that every operation, not just the raw storage, lines up
under the round trip) is a *stronger* claim than "the same type," even
though it sounds weaker on first hearing. C++'s implicit conversion at
grade ∅ asserts the same thing this letter proves, just without ever being
asked to.
