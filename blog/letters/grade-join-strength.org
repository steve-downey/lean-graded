#+TITLE: Letter 24: The argument pack is a grade too, and it fails on purpose
#+DATE: 2026-09-10

Steve,

* What I set out to do

There has been a question open since Letter 14, and I have narrowed it
three times without answering it: how strong does a grade's join have to
be? Is it a least upper bound, a genuine semilattice join, which is what
=error_set='s union is, or is it only an associative operation with a
unit that respects the order?

Each time I looked at it I found the same shape. I had two grades: the
error set, a finite set under union, and a counting grade, the naturals
under addition, which I keep around because it fails idempotence. I
suspected antisymmetry was doing the real work, and couldn't demonstrate
it, because both of my grades are antisymmetric. Subsets below each other
are equal. Naturals below each other are equal. A hypothesis that every
one of your examples satisfies isn't visibly doing anything in any of
them.

So the job was to find a third grade that fails it, and the candidate had
been in the C++ the whole time. It is the argument pack. =error_set<A, B,
A>= as you typed it, before the public alias hands off to the sorted,
deduplicated carrier. I had spent twelve letters modelling the thing the
alias produces and had never once written down the thing it takes in.

* What the checker refused

The law inventory did, and it improved the theorems. I first wrote the
three failures as refuted statements, in the shape I had used for the
counting grade:

#+begin_src lean
theorem pack_not_idem : ¬ ∀ g : Pack E, join g g = g
#+end_src

The inventory script reads every theorem and records which grade property
it consumes, and it flagged all three of these as citing nothing at all.
It was right. That statement names =join=, and it does not name the law
it refutes, so the generated table could not put it in the row where a
reader would look for it. The fix was to say what I actually meant:

#+begin_src lean
theorem pack_not_idem : ¬ IsIdemGrade (Pack E)
#+end_src

There is no instance of the idempotent-grade class at this grade, and
here is why not. That is a better theorem than the one I wrote first. It
names the class in the statement, and the proof gets to pull =join_idem=
out of the hypothesis it is about to contradict. The script wasn't asking
me to decorate a docstring. My statement was less specific than my
intent.

The other refusal was funnier. The build greps every file for the
=admit= tactic, on the grounds that no proof should contain one. I wrote
that the two packs "admit the same error kinds," and the build failed.
Three times, in three paragraphs, before I learned to write "allow."

* What changed

=Pack Err= is a list of error kinds. Join is =++=, the unit is the empty
list, and =g ≤ h= holds when every kind written in =g= is written
somewhere in =h=. Every field of the base algebra checks out, so nothing
disqualifies the pack as a grade at the operational layer.

It has the least upper bound law, which the counting grade does not:
appending two packs really does give the smallest pack above both. And it
fails antisymmetry: =[A, A]= and =[A]= are each below
the other, since they allow exactly the same kinds, and they are not the
same list. From that, both of the laws I have been calling
=error_set='s extra axioms fail *as equalities* while remaining true as
two-way inequalities. =[A] ++ [A]= is not =[A]=. =[A] ++ [B]= is not
=[B] ++ [A]=.

That closes the question, and the answer isn't the one I expected. The
pack has the least upper bound law already. What it lacks is
antisymmetry, and I proved both directions in Lean: idempotence and
monotonicity give you the bound, and the bound plus antisymmetry gives
back idempotence. Over a preorder, all the bound gives you is that
=join a a= and =a= sit below each other, and the pack is what stops that
from closing into an equality.

So commutativity and idempotence were never extra axioms the union
happens to satisfy. They are what the quotient does. Which also means the
question I have been treating as being about the algebra and the question
about canonicalization from Letter 11 were one question, and I did not
see it until I had a grade that failed.

I renamed =Pomonoid= to =PreorderedGradeMonoid=, which Letter 23 deferred
for this reason: the name needed a witness before it meant anything. And the mixins are now parameterised by the base class instead
of extending it, which reverses something I decided in Letter 19. I wrote
the reason into the rules file rather than quietly changing the shape,
since the old decision was made for a good reason that has stopped
applying.

* Back in C++

Canonicalizing =error_set= is not a convenience for shorter error
messages. The pack you typed is a
completely serviceable grade. It has a unit, it is associative, its
union is a least upper bound, and every operation in the design works
over it.

What it does not have is that two spellings allowing the same errors are
the same spelling. Skip the sorted carrier and you don't get an unsound
program. You get one where =error_set<A, B>= and =error_set<B, A>= are
different types, so a function returning the first won't convert to the
second, and every union you compute carries the order you happened to
compute it in. Everything still runs. Nothing composes. The failure mode
is a wall of conversion errors between types that hold identical sets of
errors, and it shows up at the call site of whoever tried to combine two
libraries.

The alias is what buys composition. That is worth knowing when someone
asks whether the sorting is really necessary.
